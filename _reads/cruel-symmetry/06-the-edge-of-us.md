Part 5 was one half of the answer. When you can't trust a powerful thing to see
its own limits, you *limit what it can reach.* You shrink the damage from the
outside. Keys, not keyrings.

But there's a second half, and it works from the inside. Instead of only
shrinking what a narrow self can wreck, you can try to make the self *less
narrow.* And that turns on a question that runs underneath almost everything:
**who counts as "us"?**

Every religion, every nation, every movement, every culture is — among other
things — a machine for answering that one question. Where do you draw the circle?
Some people draw it tight, around just themselves. Some around their family.
Their tribe. Their country. All of humanity. All living things. And where you
draw that circle changes nearly everything about how you behave — *without
changing how smart or capable you are at all.*

There are really two dials. The first is *how wide* — who's inside the circle
right now. The second is *how far ahead* — does the circle include people who
aren't born yet? Someone who counts only today's self will burn tomorrow for a
reward this afternoon. Someone whose circle includes their future self saves,
plans, quits the cigarette. A civilization that counts its grandchildren as part
of "us" treats the forests and the climate very differently from one that treats
the future as a stranger's problem.

Now look back at every "villain" in this series — and notice none of them were
villains. The junior engineer who wiped the database. The narrow super-machine
that kills its own power grid. Cancer, which thrives right up until it kills the
body it lives in. Every one of them made the *same* mistake: they drew the circle
of "self" too small to include the things keeping them alive. The boundary of
"me" stopped short of the floor "I" was standing on.

The wise version is always the reverse — someone who *widened* the circle. A
clever shepherd guards the flock not out of sentiment but because, somewhere
along the way, the flock became part of how he thinks about his own future. A
wise ruler stops bleeding the kingdom dry when the kingdom stops feeling like
"them" and starts feeling like "us." Stewardship isn't mainly a rule you enforce.
It's what naturally happens when your sense of self grows big enough to include
what you depend on.

So here are the two halves of the answer, side by side. Part 5 was the
**outside-in** fix: assume the self stays narrow, and contain the damage with
limits and keys. Part 6 is the **inside-out** one: try to grow the self, so the
actor *wants* to protect more, because more of the world now counts as its own.
You need both. Limits alone give you a safe, small, suspicious world. A wide
circle with no limits trusts too much and gets burned. The grown-up answer is to
widen the circle *and* keep the keys honest.

But I have to be honest about how hard the inside-out half is, because it's the
oldest unfinished project we have. "Love your neighbor." "Love your enemy." The
Stoics who called the wise person a citizen of the whole world. The traditions
that extend compassion to every living thing. People have been trying to widen
the circle for thousands of years — and we keep backsliding. It expands in safe
times and snaps shut in frightened ones.

You also can't force it. There is no law that makes a heart bigger. What actually
moves the circle is what always has: stories, examples, shared trials, and coming
to know, as a person, someone who used to be a "them." That's the real reason
religion and culture have always been so powerful and so bitterly fought over.
They were the original technology for setting the edge of "us."

Which is exactly why this moment is so charged. The real struggle of this era may
not be "humans versus AI" at all. It may be a struggle between competing
definitions of "us" — and AI is about to become one of the loudest voices in it.
It could go either way. It could be a tribal amplifier: endless tailored outrage,
every person sealed into a smaller and smaller circle of people who already agree.
Or it could be a translation layer: something that finally helps you understand
the person on the far side of a boundary, widening the circle instead of carving
it up. The technology doesn't decide that. We do — by what we build, and how we
use it. The same choice, one more time.

And that's where the whole series lands. The Cruel Symmetry says our power will
always outrun our wisdom, because power inherits and wisdom starts over. We can't
erase that. But we have two honest moves. We can **limit the keys**, so that when
something goes blind — and it will — it can't take everything down with it. And we
can **widen the circle**, slowly and unevenly, generation by generation, so that
more of what we depend on is counted as part of ourselves.

Neither move solves the oldest problem. Anyone who tells you they've solved it has
only shown you they're standing too close to see it — which is the whole warning,
all over again. But both moves make it less deadly. And the most important move is
the quiet one beneath both: write it down. Hand the warning forward. Wisdom
doesn't inherit on its own the way tools do — so we have to inherit it on purpose.

That's not the end of the problem. It's just one more generation refusing to make
the next one start from zero.
