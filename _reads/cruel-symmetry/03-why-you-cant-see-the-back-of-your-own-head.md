Try to tickle yourself. Go ahead.

You can't. Your brain always knows the tickle is coming, because *you're* the one
doing it. There's no surprise, because the tickler and the tickled are the same
system. Some things are impossible not because you're not trying hard enough,
but because of *what you are.*

This turns out to be one of the deepest facts we know, and it's the key to our
whole puzzle.

A camera can photograph almost anything in the world — except the inside of
itself, in the act of taking the picture. A dictionary tries to define every
word using other words, but it can never step *outside* of language to show you
what a word truly points to. Look up "exist," and it sends you to other words,
which send you to other words, around and around, never quite touching the
ground. The dictionary can't get outside itself.

About a century ago, a mathematician named Kurt Gödel proved that this isn't
just a quirk of cameras and dictionaries — it's a law. He showed that any system
of logic powerful enough to be genuinely useful will always contain true things
it *cannot prove* using only its own rules. To confirm those truths, you have to
step *outside* the system, up to a bigger one. And there's a kicker: a system can
never fully prove, from the inside, that it isn't broken. To check whether your
own logic is sound, you'd need a vantage point above your own logic — which, by
definition, you don't have.

You don't need the math. You just need the shape of it, because it's the same
shape as the fish in the water and the demon who thought he was God:

**Any mind, looking only at itself, has a blind spot it cannot remove from the
inside.**

Which leads to a strange and useful test. If something ever announces, *"I am
complete. I have it all figured out. There is nothing above me,"* — that
announcement is not a sign of strength. It's the warning light. A healthy
system *knows* it can't fully verify itself, the same way a wise person knows
there are things they're missing. Only a broken system, or a foolish one, is
totally certain. The demon's boast — "there is no one above me" — wasn't proof he
was God. It was proof he wasn't.

So notice what the real danger actually is. It is *not* the not-knowing. We are
all fish in water; none of us sees the whole picture, and that's just the human
condition. The danger is something more specific: **closing the book.** Deciding
you're done learning. Mistaking your map for the whole territory and acting on it
with full force.

In this light, humility stops being a nice personality trait you're supposed to
have. It becomes simply *being correct about your own limits.* The humble person
and the wise person are the same person, because both of them have accepted the
one thing the math guarantees: there is always a level you can't see from here.

Now let's aim this at the most powerful thing we are currently building.
