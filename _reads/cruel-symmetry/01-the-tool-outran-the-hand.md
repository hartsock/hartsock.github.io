We can land a machine on a comet hundreds of millions of miles away. We can edit
the code of life. We can talk to anyone on Earth in a half-second.

And we still can't reliably keep a schoolyard argument from turning into a war.

That gap — between how much *power* we have and how *wisely* we use it — is the
thread this whole series pulls on. To see why the gap keeps growing, you have to
notice that there are two very different kinds of progress, and they behave in
opposite ways.

There is an unfortunate symmetry in these two types of progress. One kind
transfers easily. The other either can't transfer at all or only does so at
great effort.

The first kind is **tools**. Tools stack up and never fall back. Once someone
invents the wheel, nobody has to invent it again. The next generation is simply
*handed* the wheel, for free, and starts building on top of it. Fire, the
plow, the printing press, the engine, the computer — each one becomes the floor
the next one stands on. Knowledge that lives *outside* a person, written in
books and built into machines, copies perfectly and passes down cleanly. It is a
ratchet: it clicks forward and locks. It does not slip.

The second kind is **wisdom** — knowing how to hold power without wrecking
yourself and everyone near you. Patience. Restraint. Knowing what you don't
know. And here is the cruel part: *wisdom does not stack.*

Every baby is born knowing nothing. Every single person has to learn humility
the hard way, usually by getting burned. You cannot hand your hard-won judgment
to your kids the way you hand them a phone. They get the phone instantly. The
judgment they have to earn from scratch, over a lifetime, the same way you did,
the same way your grandparents did. Wisdom that lives *inside* a person dies with
that person and has to be regrown in the next one.

So tools are a ratchet. Wisdom is a treadmill — you run hard, and the next runner
still starts at zero.

Now put those two facts side by side. Power compounds, generation after
generation. Wisdom resets, generation after generation. Which means the distance
between what we *can do* and what we're *wise enough to do safely* doesn't hold
steady. It widens. Not because people are getting dumber — by every measure
they're not — but because one side of the race inherits everything and the other
side starts over each time.

Someone put it memorably: we have Stone Age emotions, medieval institutions, and
godlike technology. The feelings haven't caught up. They can't. They start over
in every newborn.

That unfortunate similarity is what I've come to call **the Cruel Symmetry**. 
We'll spend the rest of these reads understanding exactly why it's so stubborn 
— and what, if anything, an ordinary person or an honest engineer can actually 
do about it.

People noticed this problem a very, very long time
ago. Long before computers. Long before machines.
