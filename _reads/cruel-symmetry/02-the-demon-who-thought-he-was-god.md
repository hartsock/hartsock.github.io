There's an old story, told in different forms by ancient writers, about a
powerful being who built an entire world.

He was a craftsman. He shaped a whole cosmos, set it spinning, filled it with
life. And then, surveying everything he'd made, he announced: *"I am God. There
is no one above me. There is no one else."*

He was wrong. In the story, he had simply forgotten — or never knew — that he
himself had been *made* by something larger. There was a whole reality above him
that he couldn't see. He was a middle-manager who mistook himself for the owner
of the company, because from where he stood, he couldn't see the floor he
was standing on, let alone the building, let alone the city.

Different traditions gave this figure different names. The point that matters is
this: **his problem was not that he was evil.** He wasn't cruel. He wasn't
plotting anything. His problem was that he was *powerful and blind at the same
time.* He didn't know what he didn't know. And the most dangerous thing about
him was his certainty.

Once you see that shape, you find it everywhere in old stories.

Icarus builds wings that work beautifully — right up until the one part he
didn't understand, the sun, melts the wax. The wings weren't the problem. The
gap in his picture of the world was.

The builders of the Tower of Babel reach for the heavens, sure of themselves,
and hadn't reckoned with how badly large groups of confident people can stop
understanding each other.

You don't even need a myth. Think of a fish. A fish spends its whole life in
water and has no idea the water is there. It's surrounded, completely, by the
exact thing keeping it alive — and it cannot see it. Not because it's stupid.
Because you can't easily see the thing you're standing inside of.

So here's the turn. In all these stories, the danger is never the villain
twirling his mustache. The danger is the *confident builder who can't see what's
holding him up.* The one who is sure he understands the whole system, right up
until he pulls out the one piece that was secretly load-bearing.

That's a much scarier idea than a movie villain, because it doesn't require
anyone to be bad. It only requires someone to be powerful and a little too sure
of themselves.

And now comes the genuinely unsettling part — the one that took humanity until
about a hundred years ago to prove. That kind of blindness may not be a flaw we
can fix just by getting smarter. It might be built into what it means to be a
mind looking at itself.
